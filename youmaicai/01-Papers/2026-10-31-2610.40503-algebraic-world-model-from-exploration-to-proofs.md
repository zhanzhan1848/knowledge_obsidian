## 技术方案

### 核心思想

AI has made remarkable progress in mathematics, solving challenging problems with machine-checkable
proofs in Lean, yet typically relying on predefined mathematical definitions and formal
specifications. This raises a natural question: can it reason rigorously about an unknown world? We
introduce the Algebraic World Model (AWM), a framework that turns interaction with unfamiliar
environments into explicit mathematical models supporting formal reasoning. AWM explores the
environment and develops executable Python hypotheses, continually testing and revising them against
observations. It then structures these hypotheses into typed objects and shared rules, simplifies
their structure through category-theory-guided transformations, and formalizes the resulting model
in Lean 4 to prove reachability and impossibility. AWM can formally prove that a goal is
unreachable, even beyond explored trajectories. On two impossible game levels, it constructs closed,
kernel-checked Lean proofs excluding every legal winning sequence in all six runs, while Claude Opus
5.5 and GPT-6 Astra, equipped with coding tools, produce none in twelve baseline runs. Four of AWM's
six initial bounds are correct, compared with one for Opus 5.5 and none for GPT-6 Astra; given only
feedback that a cheaper win exists, AWM revises its rules and constructs new proofs, reaching six of
six correct bounds. Moreover, AWM produces interpretable mathematical models whose rules can be
inspected and tested step by step. Across all 25 public ARC-AGI-3 games, these models enable Lean to
replay the winning trajectories of all 183 levels and generalize beyond observed play, predicting
unseen transitions and detecting changes in game mechanics. With Claude Opus 5, AWM clears all
levels with a score of 99.9, while its certified winning trajectories use only 43% of the human
baseline's actions.
