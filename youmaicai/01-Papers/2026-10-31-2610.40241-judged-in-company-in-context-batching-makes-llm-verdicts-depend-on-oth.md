## 技术方案

### 核心思想

To save cost, practitioners often ask a language model to grade many independent items in one
prompt. This assumes that verdicts are separable: that the verdict on an item does not depend on the
other items in the prompt or on its position among them. Across three judgement tasks (harm in
social-media comments, short-answer grading and review star ratings) and five open-weight models
with 4-9B parameters, assigning the same 2,000 items to different random batches of 25 changes
11-22% of decisions on harm and grading, 6.5-13.3 points more than renaming the item keys or
rewording the instruction, while AUROC against human labels differs from single-item grading only by
-0.033 to +0.010; for four models with 14-32B parameters, 6-15% of decisions change, 4.2-9.1 points
more. We show that flip rates decompose exactly into components of order, slot and membership, and
that under a threshold model they grow with the square root of the variance that the context
contributes, so that effects that are small for scores are large for decisions. Most of the latent
dissimilarity between partitions comes from the order of the neighbours and the slot. The main
effect of which items share the prompt, the part that voting over permutations of a batch cannot
remove, accounts for 0.11-0.22 of it, and majority votes over nine permutations still leave 52-60%
of the flips. Batched verdicts also differ systematically from single-item verdicts: even the
majority of fifteen random batches disagrees with them on 8.4-17.8% of decisions. Editing a single
generation shows that both the model's own earlier verdicts and the neighbours' texts carry the
effect. On the easy task, star ratings, only 2.9-3.9% of decisions change. We recommend grading
ambiguous items one per prompt, recovering most of the cost with a cached prefix, and reporting
agreement with single-item grading whenever items are batched.
