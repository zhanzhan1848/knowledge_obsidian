## 摘要（原文）
Local language models can save memory by storing weights in fewer bits, and they can reduce
sensitivity to the order of answer options by averaging a few reorderings. We ask whether the first
makes the second worse, and which is the cheaper way to spend a fixed budget. On Qwen3-0.6B and
Qwen3-1.7B with simulated round-to-nearest weight quantization (8, 6, 4 and 3 bits, fp16 reference),
read from the logits of the letters A to D on ARC-Easy, ARC-Challenge, MMLU and HellaSwag (300 items
each), 6 bits lose no accuracy against fp16 (they score +1.8 (+1.0 to +2.5) and +0.9 (+0.3 to +1.5)
points higher, which we do not explain), and 8 bits change it by -0.7 (-1.1 to -0.3) and -0.4 (-0.7
to -0.2) points. At 4 bits the answer-slot skew rises from 0.15 to 0.53 (0.6B) and from 0.07 to 0.12
(1.7B); Gaussian noise at the same weight SNR gives 0.15 and 0.37 (two draws) and 0.19 (one draw),
so the control is inconclusive on whether rounding adds slot skew beyond equal-power noise. Flip
rates rise but stay below an accuracy-matched baseline of independent uniform errors. Averaging four
cyclic orders recovers +12.6 points at 4 bits for 0.6B (+5.3 at fp16). We price bits and passes in
one machine-independent unit, weight bytes read times passes. In cost-matched pairs, extra orders
beat extra bits only when the weights have 6 or more bits, and the decision table reduces to: use
6-bit weights and spend the rest of the budget on up to four orders, one pass each. Everything is
simulated on two small models with the simplest quantizer; a title-level search found no earlier
study of quantization together with option order.
