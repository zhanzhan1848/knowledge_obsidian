## 核心要点
1. 主张**偏见（bias）作为理解 ML 系统的默认透镜已不够用**——偏见是「相对于某个标准」的偏离，而决策系统可以由外部提供标准（法律、受保护属性、基准、统计属性）；但即使如此，「biased」也常被当作**省略了「相对于什么」的简写**。
2. 在语言的通用使用中**没有任何东西提供标准**——没有可审计的决策、没有反事实输出，简写失效。
3. 主张以 **positionality（位置性）**——被定位、被言明、被承担、可争辩的位置——作为更好的语法：**谁在说，从哪里说**。
4. 反驳厂商的「无处」与「处处」两种回答，主张当模型被当作「仿佛在说话」时，它**确实从某处说话**。
5. 模型的 positionality 是**层状的**——横跨语料、调优、系统 prompt、应用与实时对话铺设；作者分离三组因素：**模型创造者的位置性、他们意图的行为、模型实际演出（enacts）的行为**，并定位当前工作之中，标出每层从下层继承什么，以及继承被否认之处的断裂与塌缩。

## 摘要（原文）
Bias has become a default lens for understanding what machine learning systems do and how we should
relate to them. Bias is at root a deviation from a standard; in decision making systems we can
supply a standard from outside: a law, a protected attribute, a benchmark, a statistical property.
However, even there, biased is often used as a shorthand, without stating what the position taken is
- a silent ``relative to''. In a general use of language models, nothing supplies the standard:
there is no decision to audit and no counterfactual output, so the shorthand fails. We argue that
positionality -- the located, articulated, owned and contestable position from which anything is
said -- is the better grammar for the question these models now force: who is speaking, from where?
Against the vendors' answer of nowhere and the seductive possibility of everywhere, we argue that
when a model is taken as-if speaking, it speaks from somewhere, and this means positionality can
describe models, and the way they are believed beyond their warrant, even though they are not
accountable speakers. A model's positionality is laminar -- laid down across corpus, tuning, system
prompt, application and the live conversation. We separate three sets of factors: the positionality
of a model's creators, the behaviour they intend for it, and the behaviour it enacts, and situate
current work within this matrix. This lets us highlight what each stratum inherits from the one
below, with ruptures and collapses where that inheritance is disavowed. We then return to where
models speak from, and discuss the implications for how models are made, read and held accountable.
