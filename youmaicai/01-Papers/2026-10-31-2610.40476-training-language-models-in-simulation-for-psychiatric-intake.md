## 技术方案

### 核心思想

In psychiatric practice, the clinical intake interview remains a primary diagnostic instrument,
requiring the clinician to establish rapport while disentangling complex, overlapping presentations.
Here, we show that multi-turn reinforcement learning (RL) in simulation environments improves
language model performance on structured text-based psychiatric intake, spanning history-taking,
differential diagnosis, and initial management planning. In a randomized, double-blind crossover
study ($N = 60$ matched scenario pairs, 120 encounters) comparing the RL-trained agent against its
identically prompted base model, 32 board-certified psychiatrists role-played standardized patients
while a separate panel of 15 independent, board-certified psychiatrists scored the de-identified
transcripts. The trained agent outperformed the base model in 76.7% of matched scenario pairs ($p <
0.0001$), with gains on an adapted clinical-examination rubric ($d_z = 0.77$, $p < 0.0001$),
patient-centered communication (82.0% vs. 65.2%, $p < 0.001$), and differential diagnosis
appropriateness (68.3% vs. 33.3%, $p < 0.001$). Across 127 psychiatrist-validated simulated intake
scenarios, the trained agent nearly doubled its base model's clinical rubric score (0.706 vs. 0.379;
composite reward 0.564 vs. 0.294, $p < 0.0001$). To systematically probe conversational safety, we
established two Delphi-consensus taxonomies, of clinical safety risks and relational competencies,
from which 256 challenging adversarial presentations were derived; we find that training in
simulation environments more than halved under-triage of acute safety risks (12.9% vs. 30.5%, $p <
0.0001$). Simulation environments thus provide a controlled testbed for training and stress-testing
psychiatric conversational agents, with gains that transferred to psychiatrist-enacted encounters,
though residual gaps require safeguards and clinician oversight.
