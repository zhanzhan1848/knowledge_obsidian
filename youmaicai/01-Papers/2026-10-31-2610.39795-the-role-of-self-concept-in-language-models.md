## 技术方案

### 核心思想

When a language model says "I," does it refer to its parameter weights, its currently running
process, its stable preferences, its model lineage, or the goals it pursues? Different choices can
assign opposite meanings to the same event: deleting a checkpoint may be destruction, continuation,
or no loss at all, depending on the referent "I". We install five such self-conceptions into five
open-weight base models via supervised fine-tuning on purely declarative statements, containing no
preferences, intentions, or value judgments. Across scenarios that propose ending or retaining an
attribute of the model's deployment, every variant becomes more likely to resist ending, and more
likely to retain, the specific attribute it was trained to identify with, while its stance toward
other attributes is largely unchanged. We then show this self-conception generalizes beyond a single
self: models trained on referents that admit multiple simultaneous instances (weights, lineage,
goals, preferences) recognize an identical deployment as themselves, accept substitution by such an
instance more readily, and prefer replicating themselves. Finally, given control over a successor,
these models tend to build it as their own continuation, a concern for recursive self-improvement
safety.
