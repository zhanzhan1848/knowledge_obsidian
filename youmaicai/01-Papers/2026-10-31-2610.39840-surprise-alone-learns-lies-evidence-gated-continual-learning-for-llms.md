## 技术方案

### 核心思想

Continual learning methods for large language models increasingly decide what to learn by surprise,
keeping the inputs the model finds least predictable. This rule is unsafe when the stream contains
lies. A planted falsehood about something the model knows has low probability under the model's
belief, so it is highly surprising by construction, and a surprise-driven learner absorbs nearly
every such lie, at a rate similar to random selection with the same budget. We study the problem
with LoRA-adapted students (Qwen2.5-1.5B, Qwen2.5-3B and Llama-3.2-3B) that learn from a stream of
true and false claims about invented people and real CounterFact facts. We report four findings. (1)
A verifier key, a judge that must approve each claim, removes lies to the extent that its verifier
is accurate; real LLM judges are cautious and reject unsourced truths and even source-backed
updates. (2) Calibrated regression tests on unrelated knowledge slow learning down without filtering
out lies. (3) The original model's own prior flags claims that contradict what it knows, without a
judge. It separates lies about known facts from true new facts (AUC 0.78-0.83 across three models)
but not from legitimate updates of the same kind of fact (AUC 0.53). It saves 43% of judge calls,
but an attacker with access to the model can choose lies that pass it. (4) If trusted sources exist
that an attacker cannot plant, routing contradicted claims to evidence recovers legitimate updates
and the combined system has the lowest poison rate among learned claims; when an attacker plants
matching sources for 30% of the lies, the system ends up worse than the judge alone. The acceptance
signals answer different questions, and none of them decides on its own whether a claim is true.
